# Content Strategy Framework

## Intent
Use Bharat Anand's The Content Trap framework to build a content strategy that avoids the three "better content" traps and instead leverages bundling, linking, and network effects to create defensible advantages.

## Inputs
- Your content assets (existing and planned)
- Target audience and their content consumption patterns
- Competitive content landscape
- Business model (subscription, advertising, transactional)
- Distribution channels and partnerships

## Process (SOP)
1. Step 1: Identify which content trap you are falling into — trap 1 (better content: competing on quality alone), trap 2 (more content: volume as strategy), or trap 3 (mimicking successful competitors' content).
2. Step 2: Audit your current content against the three traps — are you investing in quality improvements, volume increases, or imitation without a structural advantage?
3. Step 3: Shift from content quality thinking to content connection thinking — how does your content link to other content, products, or experiences to create value greater than the sum of parts?
4. Step 4: Design a bundled content strategy — combine complementary content types (e.g., education + community + tools) so the bundle is more valuable than any standalone piece.
5. Step 5: Map your linking architecture — define how content pieces connect to each other (internal linking, sequential journeys, cross-references) to increase engagement and switching costs.
6. Step 6: Identify network effects in your content strategy — does each new user/subscriber make the content more valuable for others? (e.g., reviews, discussions, UGC, data network effects).
7. Step 7: Evaluate subscription and bundling opportunities — can you bundle content with other products or services to increase willingness to pay and reduce churn?
8. Step 8: Analyze your competitors' content linkages as well as their content quality — where are the gaps in their network that you can exploit?
9. Step 9: Prioritize investments in linking and bundling over standalone content quality improvements — a well-linked average-content strategy outperforms an isolated premium-content strategy.
10. Step 10: Measure the impact of connections — track metrics like cross-content engagement, bundle uptake, and churn rate among users who consume linked content vs. isolated content.

## Outputs
- A content trap audit identifying which traps you are vulnerable to
- A bundled content architecture with complementary offerings
- A content linking map showing connection pathways
- A prioritized investment plan favoring linking/bundling over quality alone
- A measurement framework for connection-driven metrics

## Evidence
Source: "The Content Trap" by Bharat Anand. Key concepts: three content traps (better, more, mimicking), bundling strategy, linking architecture, network effects in content, connection over quality, subscription bundling, switching costs, content ecosystem design.

## Examples
A business education publisher stops competing on content quality alone. They bundle courses with templates, community access, and expert Q&A. They create a linking architecture where each course references case studies from the community. Churn drops 30% and willingness to pay increases because the bundle is more valuable than any single course.

## Metrics
- Cross-content engagement rate (users consuming multiple linked pieces)
- Bundle vs. standalone uptake ratio
- Churn rate: linked content consumers vs. isolated content consumers
- Network effect indicator (value per user growth as user base grows)
- Content connection density (average links per content piece)

## Anti-Patterns
- Competing on content quality alone in a market where competitors have equal or greater resources
- Producing more content without increasing connections or bundle value
- Copying a competitor's content strategy without building your own linking architecture
- Treating each content piece as a standalone asset instead of part of an ecosystem
- Investing in premium production quality when the marginal return is lower than investing in connectivity
- Ignoring network effects — the most defensible content strategies become more valuable as they grow
- Measuring content success with vanity metrics (page views, downloads) instead of connection and retention metrics
