# Acquisition Readiness Audit

## Intent
Use Andrew Gazdecki's Getting Acquired framework to prepare your company for a successful acquisition — identify likely buyers, maximize valuation drivers, clean your cap table, and organize due diligence materials so you can respond within days.

## Inputs
- Current cap table and shareholder structure
- Financial statements (P&L, balance sheet, cash flow) for 3 years
- Customer metrics (ARR, churn, CAC, LTV, NRR)
- Intellectual property documentation (patents, trademarks, code ownership)
- Contracts with key customers, partners, and employees
- Corporate governance records (board minutes, equity issuances)

## Process (SOP)
1. Step 1: Identify your most likely acquirers — map strategic buyers (companies that would benefit from owning your product), financial buyers (PE firms), and acqui-hire candidates; prioritize by strategic fit and ability to pay.
2. Step 2: Analyze each potential buyer's acquisition history — what have they bought before, at what multiples, and what integration patterns do they follow?
3. Step 3: Assess your valuation drivers — recurring revenue growth rate, gross margin, net revenue retention, market position, and proprietary technology; identify which drivers are strong and which need improvement.
4. Step 4: Clean your cap table — resolve any outstanding equity disputes, convert SAFEs or notes, ensure all shareholder agreements are current, and eliminate any unusual terms that could scare buyers.
5. Step 5: Audit your intellectual property — confirm all IP is assigned to the company (not founders personally), all open-source dependencies are compliant, and all contractor work has proper IP assignment agreements.
6. Step 6: Organize financial documentation — prepare audited or reviewed financials, clean up any related-party transactions, and ensure revenue recognition is defensible.
7. Step 7: Review all material contracts — customer agreements, vendor contracts, employment agreements, and leases — for change-of-control provisions that could complicate or block an acquisition.
8. Step 8: Build a data room — create a secure, organized repository of all due diligence materials (financials, legal, HR, IP, customer, and operational documents) so you can respond to buyer requests within days, not weeks.
9. Step 9: Address red flags proactively — customer concentration risk, founder dependency, technical debt, and regulatory exposure are the top deal-killers; mitigate them before entering discussions.
10. Step 10: Engage a qualified M&A advisor or investment banker who understands your market and can run a competitive process to maximize valuation.

## Outputs
- A prioritized buyer target list with strategic rationale
- A valuation driver assessment with improvement roadmap
- A clean, buyer-ready cap table
- A fully organized virtual data room
- A red flag mitigation plan
- An engaged M&A advisor relationship

## Evidence
Source: "Getting Acquired" by Andrew Gazdecki. Key concepts: buyer identification and mapping, valuation drivers (growth rate, margins, retention), cap table hygiene, IP assignment and compliance, due diligence data room preparation, change-of-control provisions, customer concentration risk, competitive M&A process.

## Examples
A vertical SaaS company with $8M ARR prepares for acquisition. They clean up a messy cap table (3 unresolved SAFEs), assign all contractor IP to the company, and build a data room. They identify 12 strategic buyers, engage a banker, and run a competitive process — selling at 8x ARR vs. the 5x they would have gotten without preparation.

## Metrics
- Cap table cleanliness score (no unresolved instruments, no disputes)
- Data room completeness (all requested documents available within 48 hours)
- Valuation driver improvement (ARR growth rate, gross margin, NRR trend)
- Number of qualified buyer conversations generated
- Time from first buyer contact to signed LOI

## Anti-Patterns
- Waiting to prepare until a buyer approaches — by then you have no leverage and no time
- Ignoring cap table messiness — it is the number one deal-killer in early diligence
- Overlooking IP assignment from contractors and early employees
- Failing to address customer concentration (one customer >20% of revenue is a red flag)
- Running a single-buyer process with no competitive tension
- Neglecting to review change-of-control clauses in key contracts until the buyer's lawyers find them
- Presenting inflated or unverifiable financials — sophisticated buyers will discover discrepancies and walk away
